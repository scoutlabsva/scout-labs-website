If you run a small service business, you've probably heard that AI and automation can save you time. What's harder to find is a straight answer to the obvious next question: *where do I actually start?*

The honest answer is that you shouldn't try to automate everything, or even most things. The best first project is usually small, a little boring, and something your team does every single week. This guide walks through how to find it.

## Start with the work behind the work

Most owners don't lose time to the work they're good at, like fixing the furnace, building the deck, or preparing the tax return. They lose it to the work *around* that work:

- Retyping customer details from an email or voicemail into a schedule or job list
- Chasing estimates that never got a reply
- Copying the same information between your scheduling tool, your invoicing software, and a spreadsheet
- Renaming and filing documents that clients send in
- Pulling together the same numbers for a weekly or monthly report

None of these tasks is hard. That's exactly why they're good candidates. They follow the same steps every time, they take attention away from paying work, and when they slip, customers notice.

## A simple test: frequent, predictable, and annoying

Before you spend any money, make a quick list of recurring tasks and ask three questions about each one.

1. **How often does it happen?** A task that happens 20 times a week is a better candidate than one that happens twice a year, even if the twice-a-year task is more painful.
2. **Does it follow the same steps each time?** If you could write the steps on an index card, it can probably be automated. If every case needs judgment and a conversation, it probably shouldn't be, at least not fully.
3. **What happens when it's late or forgotten?** A missed follow-up can mean a lost job. A late internal report is just irritating. Prioritize the tasks where slipping costs you customers or money.

The task that scores well on all three is usually your first project.

## Good first projects for most service businesses

These come up again and again in owner-led businesses. They're good starting points because they use tools you likely already have and they're easy to check.

### 1. Getting new inquiries into one place

Requests arrive by web form, email, phone, and text, and someone has to gather them up. A simple automation can collect them into one list, such as a spreadsheet, your CRM, or your job management software, with the name, contact details, and request already filled in. Nothing gets lost in an inbox, and you can see at a glance what's waiting.

### 2. Following up on estimates

Many businesses send a good estimate and then never follow up, simply because nobody had time. An automatic, friendly check-in a few days later, and a reminder to you if there's still no answer, is one of the simplest ways to stop good leads from going quiet.

### 3. Entering the same information once

If your team types the same customer or job details into two or three different systems, connecting those systems is often worth more than any new software. Information entered once shows up everywhere it's needed.

### 4. Sorting incoming documents

For offices that handle a lot of paperwork, AI is now quite good at reading a document, working out what it is, pulling out key details, and filing it. The important part is that anything it isn't sure about gets flagged for a person to check.

## What *not* to automate first

Some things are better left alone, at least at the start.

- **Anything you do rarely.** If it happens a few times a year, a written checklist is usually better than an automation.
- **A process that isn't working yet.** Automating a messy process just makes the mess happen faster. Fix the steps first, then automate them.
- **Conversations that need judgment.** Pricing an unusual job, handling a complaint, or delivering bad news should stay with a person. AI can help draft or summarize, but a person should decide what goes out.
- **Anything you couldn't check.** If you wouldn't be able to tell whether the automation got it right, it's not a good first project.

Sometimes the right answer isn't technology at all. A better template, a shared calendar, or a clearer handoff between two people can solve the problem for free.

## Use the tools you already have

You usually don't need to replace your software to get started. Most common small-business tools, including email, Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, QuickBooks, popular scheduling and field service apps, and many CRMs, can already share information with each other. The first step is often connecting what you have, not buying something new.

Keep the first project small enough that you can see whether it's working within a few weeks. If it is, you'll have a much clearer idea of what to tackle next.

## A one-week exercise to find your starting point

If you're not sure where your time goes, try this for one normal week:

1. Keep a notepad or a note on your phone.
2. Every time you or someone on your team does something repetitive, like retyping, copying, chasing, or filing, jot it down with a tally mark.
3. At the end of the week, circle the three items with the most tally marks.
4. For each one, ask the three questions above: frequent, predictable, and costly when missed?

Whatever comes out on top is almost certainly a better place to start than whatever AI tool is in the news this month.
